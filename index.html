three people, three partial views

Three people decide whether a teenager masters algebra: the student , the teacher and the parent . Each sees only part of the picture.

the student

sees
The problem in front of them.
misses
Why they are stuck, and what to practice.

the teacher

sees
Answers from 150 students across five classes, usually after it is too late to help.
misses
Which error type, and which prerequisite is behind it, early enough to act.

the parent

sees
A grade, often weeks later.
misses
What their child is working on, and how to help tonight.

Most AI tools in schools serve one of these people in isolation. Treo keeps one learner model and gives each of them a view of it.

one model, three views

Every exchange updates one learner model: an estimate of the student’s mastery of each concept on a standards-aligned prerequisite graph. The tutor, the teacher’s copilot and the parent’s guide read the same model, so they never contradict each other. Each school runs its own isolated instance.

Each student carries a mastery estimate between 0 and 1 for every concept. After each exchange, a separate assessment step writes an evidence record, and the server caps how far any estimate can move at 0.15.

Figure 1 of the whitepaper, redrawn.

Every STEM concept is a node, linked to what must be mastered first and aligned to Common Core mathematics and NGSS science. A wrong slope-intercept answer may really be slope, two-step equations or negative numbers. The graph says where to look; the student’s evidence says which.

Treo never gives the final answer to a problem in progress. It asks the next question, reveals one step at a time and checks understanding, grounded in the school’s approved curriculum.

Students reason on paper. Treo reads photographs of handwritten work today; spoken explanations and generated visuals such as number lines and force diagrams are on the roadmap.

Each school gets its own instance with its own data store and encryption keys. Student data stays there, is never pooled across schools, is never used for advertising, and is deleted at contract end.

The school decides what parents see: learning guidance, never raw transcripts or scores.

the prototype today

A working prototype, built on Claude, runs the full loop on a 10-concept graph of algebra and Newton’s second law. Pick Mandy’s answer, then switch views: one model updates the tutor, the teacher’s copilot, the parent’s guide and the learner model at the top of this page.

It uses fictional students and stores no data. Per-school deployment, the full grade 8–12 graph, voice input and a class dashboard are on the roadmap.

The five-year figures are projections, not forecasts: partner schools × 500 students × the blended license price, plus onboarding. Spending runs ahead of revenue until year four, funded by grants, venture competitions and a seed round in 2028.

  • Existing federal grants. Department of Education guidance (July 2025) confirms formula and discretionary funds may pay for AI-enhanced high-impact tutoring.
  • Competitive grants. The April 2026 final priority on AI names adaptive learning, tutoring and virtual teaching assistants.
  • Families. From January 2027, scholarships funded by the federal scholarship tax credit can cover qualified K–12 expenses in participating states.
  • National priority. Executive Order 14277 directs that educators be equipped to use AI to improve outcomes.
